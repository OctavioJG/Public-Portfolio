From Field Research to Product Direction

Planning and leading user research with product teams, then turning findings into maps and personas that informed product decisions.

← Research & StrategyCase study

The brief

Working closely with product managers and UX designers, I helped teams investigate how people used existing software and where proposed changes might help. Product managers brought domain knowledge and hypotheses about how the product should evolve. I turned those hypotheses into research questions and designed sessions to test them against users’ day-to-day work.

Planning and running the sessions

I worked with client stewardship, sales, and product marketing to identify and recruit participants. We looked for a mix of newer and experienced users so the research could capture differences in how people learned the software, used it routinely, and handled more complex tasks. I coordinated scheduling and logistics directly with participants for both in-person and remote sessions.

I wrote a common interview guide so we could compare responses across sessions while leaving room for follow-up questions. Depending on the study, I prepared UX/UI testing scenarios, A/B comparisons, or mapping exercises. Interviews helped me understand how participants described their work. Usability testing and shadowing let me see where they hesitated, switched tools, or worked around a gap. In co-design sessions, participants could respond to early ideas and suggest alternatives.

Using maps to test assumptions

In one study, I brought a draft workflow map into interviews and asked participants to walk through their work against it. They corrected the sequence, identified people missing from the map, and showed where information changed hands. I revised the map as those differences emerged. The updated version gave the product team a clearer view of the workflow and the exceptions it needed to account for.

In another study, I combined usability testing, interviews, shadowing, and co-design to map an existing experience. Seeing the work in context revealed steps that happened outside the software. The journey map brought those steps together with the problems users encountered in the tool, giving Product and Design a shared reference for prioritizing improvements.

Building personas around meaningful differences

I compared how participants worked across roles and settings, looking at differences in experience, comfort with data, and the information people needed to make decisions. Those patterns informed two personas in one study. The personas helped the team consider where users needed more guidance and where experienced users needed flexibility. Those distinctions informed feature priorities and how information was organized.

Bringing findings into product planning

I shared emerging findings with Product, Design, and Engineering as the research progressed. Early reviews gave engineers a chance to assess feasibility while I was still synthesizing the evidence. I then brought the findings together in reports, presentations, and recommendations that informed roadmap priorities and agile planning.

One recurring issue surfaced across sessions. After submitting work, users had to follow up manually to find out where it stood. I documented that gap in the journey map and brought it to the product team as an opportunity. The team validated the need, then built and deployed a feature that let users check progress directly.

Project Credits

Collaboration
