Patient Feedback Through Co-Design

Co-designing ways for children and young people to give feedback on hospital care, and mapping how staff could act on what they shared.

← Research & StrategyCase study

The brief

The existing feedback tools were primarily designed for adults. Their language and format made it harder for some younger patients to share their experiences. Our product team partnered with a children’s hospital to understand what would make feedback easier to give and more meaningful to young people.

We also needed to understand what happened after feedback was submitted. The hospital’s patient experience team helped us examine how staff collected, reviewed, and shared feedback, and where they needed better support to follow up.

Planning two connected research tracks

I worked with Product Management to gather requirements and identify assumptions to test. I developed the research plan, coordinated scheduling and logistics, and supported the ethics approval process. The study ran across three sessions, beginning remotely and continuing with two on-site workshops.

We started with a remote focus group with the hospital’s patient experience team to understand its existing feedback practices. In the first on-site workshop, hospital staff explored what giving feedback could look like from a young patient’s perspective and what administrators would need to act on it. The research team also mapped how feedback moved between staff groups, capturing responsibilities, notifications, and escalation points.

Co-designing with young people

For the final workshop, the research team worked with an established youth advisory group. Hospital staff reviewed the session materials in advance and were present during the workshop.

We opened with storytelling. Participants shared experiences of being asked for feedback during care and described what had felt personal, what had been missing, and what they would change. They then generated ideas individually, developed storyboards for their preferred concepts, and presented those concepts to the group. The stories gave us context for the ideas the young people proposed.

What we learned

  • Care context mattered alongside age. Two patients of the same age could have different needs depending on why they were receiving care and what was happening when they were asked for feedback.
  • Young people wanted to know what happened next. Participants across age groups wanted to know whether someone had read their feedback and what action followed. They said this would influence whether they gave feedback again.
  • Personal relevance mattered more than novelty. Some younger participants liked animation and interactive elements. Across age groups, participants valued a tool that acknowledged their situation.
  • Timing shaped participation. Participants wanted the option to respond later if they were asked at a difficult moment. When and how the invitation arrived mattered alongside the form itself.
  • Staff needed ways to act on qualitative feedback. The administrative sessions surfaced gaps in how staff could synthesize comments, share them with the right teams, and track follow-up.

From research to product direction

We produced a workflow map and a consolidated findings report. The map documented different submission routes and showed how feedback moved through the organization. The report brought together findings across age groups, care scenarios, participant concepts, and design directions. Together, they gave the product team a reference for both the patient-facing experience and the staff workflows it needed to support.

The research informed a new feedback module for children and young people. It introduced customizable layouts, age-appropriate icons, and reusable content options.

Project Credits

My contribution

I gathered requirements and assumptions from Product Management, developed the research plan, coordinated logistics and ethics approvals, and facilitated research sessions. I synthesized the findings and developed the final report and supporting visuals.

Collaboration

Product Management helped define what the research needed to test. The hospital’s patient experience team and administrators explained how feedback was handled and helped prepare the youth workshop. Young people contributed their experiences, ideas, and storyboards. The research team designed and facilitated the co-design activities.
