When Growth Outpaces Clarity

As the organization grew, internal partners needed a clearer understanding of the creative team’s capabilities, how to engage its services, and what to expect from delivery.

Stakeholders held different assumptions about the work available and how it was produced. Uncertainty about services and request processes created barriers to collaboration.

The opportunity was to make creative services more visible and accessible through a shared reference that connected capabilities with clear delivery expectations.

Starting With Research

Research question

How might a structured, engaging service experience make creative capabilities clearer and more accessible to internal partners?

I conducted stakeholder interviews across different roles and business functions to understand how people discovered, understood, and engaged with the creative team.

The findings informed affinity mapping, internal client profiles, and current-state journey mapping. Together, these methods helped identify gaps in service awareness, uncertainty about available capabilities, and hesitation around initiating work.

The research suggested that documenting services alone would not address the problem. The format needed to support discovery and demonstrate the team’s capabilities alongside its service expectations.

My Role

I led the initiative from research and stakeholder alignment through service definition, information architecture, wireframing, design direction, and rollout.

I defined the service taxonomy and service-level parameters, developed the initial structure and wireframes in Figma, and collaborated with a UX designer on the catalogue’s development.

I remained responsible for project coordination, design reviews, iteration, team alignment, and quality assurance. I also led deployment through the organization’s intranet.

From Research to Structure

The research informed both the catalogue’s organization and the information presented within it.

Internal client profiles helped establish how different users might approach the service, what they needed to understand, and how much detail would support their decisions.

Current-state journey mapping connected these needs with the wider experience of requesting and receiving creative work. This helped position the catalogue as an entry point into the service, rather than an isolated reference document.

Rethinking the Service-Level Agreement

The catalogue brought service information and examples of creative work into one navigable experience.

Instead of separating expectations from capabilities, it presented them together: what the team could provide, what a service included, and what internal partners needed to consider before requesting it.

The visual design also had a practical purpose. The catalogue needed to demonstrate the care and clarity that internal partners could expect from the creative team.

Designing the Navigation

I organized the catalogue around three levels:

Service index

A shared entry point that introduced the available service areas.

Category view

An overview of the options within a service area, helping users narrow their request.

Service detail

A focused view combining scope, examples, delivery expectations, revision allowances, and usage considerations.

This structure supported both first-time visitors exploring the team’s capabilities and returning users looking for a specific service.

From Structure to Screen

I developed wireframes to establish the layout and navigation before detailed visual design.

These explored how to balance service navigation, descriptive content, examples, and delivery information. They provided a foundation for collaboration with the UX designer and for subsequent design reviews.

Throughout development, I reviewed the experience for clarity, consistency, and alignment with the service framework.

Making Capabilities Visible

Selected work examples were an important part of the catalogue’s approach. They helped internal partners understand the range of available services and connect service descriptions with tangible outputs.

The catalogue also made space for advisory support, showing that the creative team’s contribution extended beyond producing assets to helping partners make informed design decisions.

Rollout & Response

I led the catalogue’s deployment through the organization’s intranet, making it available as a shared reference for internal partners. Informal responses highlighted the visibility of the team’s capabilities and interest in using a similar format for other internal services.

Reflection

The project connected service design with information architecture and creative direction. Its purpose was to help people understand both what the team could offer and how to work with it.

It also reinforced the value of treating internal service communication as a designed experience, with the same attention to navigation, content, and usability as an external-facing product.
